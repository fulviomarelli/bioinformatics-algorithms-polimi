\section{Review: Exact Matching with Errors using Suffix Arrays}

Before diving into variant calling, we review an advanced read mapping concept: searching for a sequence within a reference genome using suffix arrays while allowing for a specific maximum number of mismatches (\( D_{max} \)). 

To optimize this search and avoid exploring dead-end paths, we pre-calculate a lower bound of expected errors for every suffix of our search word. 

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Lower Bound Error Array (\(D\))}: An array that stores the minimum number of mismatches expected when matching the remaining portion (suffix) of a search word against the reference string. 
\end{important}

If we are searching for a word (e.g., "hazel") in a reference string (e.g., "razle dazzle"), we start matching from the end of the search word. We check sub-strings incrementally: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Is the last letter "l" in the reference? Yes. (Minimum errors = 0)
    \item Is "el" in the reference? Yes. (Minimum errors = 0)
    \item Is "zel" in the reference? Yes. (Minimum errors = 0)
    \item Is "azel" in the reference? No. We must have at least one error. We increment our minimum error count to 1, and resume checking the next sub-string starting from the mismatch.
\end{enumerate}

When performing the actual recursive search in the suffix array, we compare our allowed errors (\( D_{max} \)) against the pre-calculated minimum errors for the remainder of the word. \sta If the minimum expected errors exceed our remaining allowed errors, we immediately terminate that search path, drastically reducing the computational search space.

\section{Applications of Read Mapping}

Once sequencing reads have been mapped and aligned to a reference genome, the alignments can be used to extract a variety of biological insights. The specific downstream application depends entirely on the experimental protocol applied to the DNA or RNA prior to sequencing.

\subsection{Alternative Sequencing Applications}

By modifying the experimental protocol before sequencing, we can target specific biological phenomena rather than just mapping the entire genomic sequence.

\begin{important}
  \textbf{ChIP-seq (Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Sequencing)}: A method used to analyze protein interactions with DNA. Instead of sequencing the entire genome, the goal is to sequence only the DNA fragments to which a specific protein of interest (e.g., a transcription factor like TP53) has bound.
\end{important}

In this protocol, proteins are cross-linked to the DNA, the DNA is fragmented, and an antibody is used to isolate only the fragments bound by the target protein. After sequencing and mapping, the reads strongly concentrate around the specific genomic regions where the protein bound, with the rest of the genome exhibiting only low-level random background noise. 

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Peak Calling}: The computational and statistical process of identifying regions in the genome with a significantly higher depth of mapped reads compared to background noise, indicating true protein-binding sites.
\end{important}

\begin{important}
  \textbf{RNA-seq (RNA Sequencing)}: A technique that sequences transcribed RNA molecules (converted to complementary DNA, or cDNA) to measure gene expression levels and analyze transcript structure.
\end{important}

Because RNA-seq measures the transcriptome, the mapped reads align almost exclusively to exonic regions (the parts of the genome that are transcribed into mature RNA). The analysis provides two main types of information:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expression levels:} Genes with higher read coverage correspond to higher expression levels in the cell.
    \item \textbf{Alternative splicing:} Reads can span splice junctions, starting in one exon and ending in another, effectively skipping the intronic regions that were spliced out. Analyzing these "split reads" allows us to reconstruct how exons are pieced together.
\end{itemize}

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Hi-C (Chromosome Conformation Capture)}: A method that identifies three-dimensional chromatin interactions by chemically fusing interacting genomic regions and sequencing the resulting artificial junctions.
\end{important}

The basic idea is to chemically fuse two distinct genomic regions that are physically interacting in 3D space, despite potentially being far apart on the linear DNA sequence (or even on different chromosomes). The DNA is shattered, and the artificial junctions are sequenced as read pairs. 

If Read 1 maps to a specific location (e.g., Chromosome 2) and its pair Read 2 maps to a completely different location (e.g., Chromosome 4), it indicates a physical interaction between those two loci. The accumulation of many such read pairs strongly indicates true physical contact, such as a regulatory loop. The output is typically visualized as a two-dimensional interaction map.

\section{Genomic Variations and Variant Calling}

The main application of Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) is identifying differences between the sequenced genome of an individual and the standard reference genome. This is heavily utilized in diagnostics, evolutionary biology, and cancer research.

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Variant Calling}: The computational process of identifying variable sites (mutations) and their genomic locations relative to a reference genome.
\end{important}

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Genotype Calling}: Determining the exact genotype for an individual at each identified variable site (e.g., homozygous reference, heterozygous variant, or homozygous variant).
\end{important}

We distinguish between two fundamentally different types of mutations based on their origin:

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Germline Mutation}: A variant that is present in the germ cells (egg or sperm) and is therefore inherited and present in virtually every cell of the resulting individual's body.
\end{important}
\sta These are responsible for hereditary disorders. \\
\textbf{Example:} Fibrodysplasia Ossificans Progressiva (FOP). This is a severe hereditary disease causing trauma-induced ossification of soft tissue (muscles turning to bone). It is driven by a specific germline point mutation in the \textit{ACVR1} gene. Because it is germline, every cell in the individual carries the blueprint for this defective receptor.

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Somatic Mutation}: A variant that arises in a non-germ cell after fertilization. It is passed only to the cellular descendants of that mutated cell. Somatic mutations are the primary drivers of cancer.
\end{important}
\sta To identify these, we must sequence both the tumor tissue and a normal control sample (e.g., blood) from the same patient. Subtracting the germline mutations found in the blood reveals the tumor-specific somatic mutations. \\
\textbf{Example:} Somatic \textit{ERBB2} or \textit{IDH1} mutations driving unchecked cell proliferation in glioblastoma.

\subsection{Types of Basic Genomic Variations}

Genomic variations range from single-base changes to massive structural rearrangements.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SNP (Single-Nucleotide Polymorphism)}: A single-base change that is relatively common in the general population (by convention, present in $\ge 1\%$ of the population).
    \item \textbf{SNV (Single-Nucleotide Variant)}: Any single-base change identified in an individual genome, regardless of population frequency.
\end{itemize}

\myimage{5_1/3.jpg}{}

\sta In medical genomics, researchers are often interested in rare SNVs that are not established SNPs, as common SNPs are unlikely to be the sole cause of a rare, severe genetic disease. There are roughly 3–4 million SNVs in a typical human genome.

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Indels}: Small insertions or deletions of one or a few nucleotides.
\end{important}

Indels can be particularly destructive if they occur within the protein-coding region of a gene. \\
\textbf{Example:} Frameshift Mutation. \\
Suppose a sequence of codons reads \texttt{CAT CAT}, encoding specific amino acids. If a single \texttt{A} is inserted, the reading frame shifts to \texttt{cAt aCA Tca}, changing every subsequent amino acid and often resulting in a truncated, non-functional protein.

\subsection{Structural Variants (SVs)}

Structural variants are massive genomic alterations, generally defined as being 1,000 base pairs (1 kbp) or larger. A typical genome has hundreds of these, averaging 250,000 nucleotides in length—much larger than an average gene.

SVs are categorized as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Balanced SVs}: The overall amount of DNA remains the same. Examples include inversions (a segment is flipped) and translocations (a segment is moved to another chromosome).
    \item \textbf{Unbalanced SVs}: The total amount of DNA changes. These are primarily \textbf{Copy Number Variants (CNVs)}, consisting of large deletions or duplications.
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluating Sequencing Confidence}

To reliably call variants, we must quantify our confidence in the sequencing data using two critical metrics: mapping quality and base quality.

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Mapping Quality}: The confidence with which a given read maps to a unique, specific genomic location. A low mapping quality indicates the read maps ambiguously to multiple highly repetitive regions.
\end{important}

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Base Quality}: The confidence with which an individual nucleotide was sequenced by the machine (e.g., based on the clarity of the optical signal for that specific base).
\end{important}

Both qualities are expressed as \textbf{PHRED quality scores}, computed using a logarithmic transformation based on the probability of error (\( p \)):
\begin{equation}
    Q_{PHRED} = -10 \log_{10} p
\end{equation}

\sta \textbf{Example:} PHRED Score Interpretation. \\
A PHRED score of $Q=20$ implies $p = 10^{-2} = 0.01$ (1\% error rate, 99\% accuracy). A PHRED score of $Q=30$ implies $p = 10^{-3} = 0.001$ (0.1\% error rate, 99.9\% accuracy).

\subsection{Variant Allele Frequency (VAF)}

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Variant Allele Frequency (VAF)}: The fraction of mapped reads at a specific genomic position that support the alternate (mutated) base rather than the reference base. Calculated as $f(b) = \frac{n-k}{n}$, where $n$ is total reads and $n-k$ is the number of reads with the variant.
\end{important}

For germline mutations, VAF clusters around expected discrete values:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Homozygous reference}: VAF $\approx 0\%$.
    \item \textbf{Heterozygous variant}: VAF $\approx 50\%$ (since one of two chromosomal copies is affected).
    \item \textbf{Homozygous variant}: VAF $\approx 100\%$ (since both chromosomal copies are affected).
\end{itemize}

\sta In cancer (somatic mutations), VAF analysis is much more complex due to \textit{tumor purity} (normal cells mixed with tumor cells) and \textit{tumor clonality} (sub-populations of tumor cells carrying different mutations). A heterozygous mutation in a tumor with 80\% purity will have an expected VAF of $\sim 40\%$. If a mutation only exists in a 5\% subclone, the VAF might be as low as 2.5\%, but could represent a critical clone responsible for therapy resistance.

\section{Algorithms for Single Nucleotide Variant Calling}

\subsection{Alignment Pileups}
To evaluate variants, mapped reads are visually and computationally stacked into a "pileup" format. 

\begin{minted}{text}
21 31587791 T 24 .,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,^],
21 31587792 G 25 .,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,^],
21 31587793 A 26 .,.,g.Gg,.G..GG,G.gG.,g,^],
21 31587794 G 27 .,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,.,^],
\end{minted}

This text-based output from \texttt{samtools} summarizes the alignment for individual genomic positions. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Each line details the chromosome, 1-based coordinate, reference base (e.g., \texttt{A} at position 31587793), and read coverage (26 reads).
    \item A dot (\texttt{.}) indicates a match to the reference base on the forward strand.
    \item A comma (\texttt{,}) indicates a match on the reverse strand.
    \item Uppercase/Lowercase letters (\texttt{ACGTN} / \texttt{acgtn}) indicate mismatches to the reference on the forward/reverse strands, respectively.
\end{enumerate}

In the snippet above, at position \texttt{31587793}, 16 reads favor \texttt{A} (reference), while 10 reads favor \texttt{G} (alternate). This roughly 50/50 split strongly suggests a heterozygous variant.

\subsection{A Naive Frequency-Based Heuristic}

Early approaches used a simple heuristic: filter out bases requiring $Q \ge 20$, compute VAF, and apply strict frequency thresholds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If VAF $< 20\% \implies$ Homozygous reference.
    \item If $20\% \le$ VAF $\le 80\% \implies$ Heterozygous variant.
    \item If VAF $> 80\% \implies$ Homozygous variant.
\end{itemize}

\sta \textbf{Limitations:} This naive approach fails severely at low sequencing depths. If a position has only 6 reads, finding 1 alternate read yields a 17\% VAF. The heuristic calls this "homozygous reference". However, due to random sampling, pulling 5 reference reads and 1 alternate read from a truly heterozygous (50/50) site is entirely possible. Setting hard read-count minimums or throwing away data below a quality threshold results in a massive loss of useful probabilistic information.

\subsection{Probabilistic Approach: The MAQ Algorithm}

To address depth and quality issues, algorithms like MAQ (Mapping and Assembly with Qualities) use Bayesian inference to model the uncertainty of both the sequencing and mapping processes.

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Bayes' Theorem}: Relates the conditional probability of a model given the data, to the likelihood of the data given the model:
  \[ P(E_i \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid E_i) P(E_i)}{\sum_i P(B \mid E_i) P(E_i)} \]
  Where $E_i$ represents the possible states (genotypes) and $B$ represents the observed data.
\end{important}

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) Estimation}: A Bayesian methodology used to generate an estimate of an unknown quantity that equals the mode of the posterior distribution. We select the genotype $g \in \{ \langle a,a \rangle, \langle a,b \rangle, \langle b,b \rangle \}$ that maximizes the posterior probability:
  \begin{equation}
      \hat{g} = \underset{g}{\operatorname{argmax}} p(g \mid D) = \underset{g}{\operatorname{argmax}} p(D \mid g) p(g)
  \end{equation}
\end{important}

\subsubsection{Integrating Mapping and Base Quality}
Before computing genotype probabilities, MAQ recalculates the base quality $Q_i$ of a nucleotide as the minimum of its original base quality and the mapping quality $Q_z$ of the read it belongs to:
\begin{equation}
    Q_i = \min(Q_i, Q_z)
\end{equation}
\textbf{Rationale:} If a base has a high sequencing quality, but the read has a terrible mapping quality (we don't know where it truly belongs in the genome), we should not trust this base. Conversely, a confident mapping does not save a poorly sequenced, blurry optical signal. Taking the minimum ensures we only trust data that is both accurately sequenced \textit{and} accurately positioned.

\subsubsection{Consensus Genotype Calling}
Assume an alignment column $D$ has $n$ total reads, with $k$ reference bases ($a$) and $n-k$ alternate bases ($b$). MAQ computes the posterior probability for all three possible genotypes and picks the maximum.

To do this, MAQ defines prior probabilities ($r$) for observing a heterozygous genotype:
\[ P(\langle a, a \rangle) = (1 - r) / 2 \]
\[ P(\langle b, b \rangle) = (1 - r) / 2 \]
\[ P(\langle a, b \rangle) = r \]

To reduce false positives, $r$ is low ($r = 0.001$) for novel mutations, but much higher ($r = 0.2$) for known SNPs. \sta This aggressive prior for new SNPs heavily penalizes false-positive heterozygous calls caused by sequencing noise.

Next, we calculate the probability of the data given the model ($P(D \mid g)$). 
If the true genotype is homozygous reference $\langle a,a \rangle$, then all $n-k$ alternate bases must be errors. MAQ defines $\alpha_{n,k}$ as the probability of observing $k$ errors in $n$ nucleotides (calculated using a modified binomial distribution factoring in individual base error rates).

The unnormalized posterior probabilities are computed as:
\begin{align}
p(g = \langle a, a \rangle \mid D) &\propto \alpha_{n, n - k} \cdot (1 - r) / 2 \\
p(g = \langle b, b \rangle \mid D) &\propto \alpha_{n, k} \cdot (1 - r) / 2 \\
p(g = \langle a, b \rangle \mid D) &\propto \binom{n}{k} \frac{1}{2^n} \cdot r
\end{align}

To normalize, MAQ divides each by the sum of all three. It then selects the genotype with the highest posterior probability and reports a genotype quality score $Q_g = -10 \log_{10}[1 - P(\hat{g} \mid D)]$.

\section{Pipeline for Calling Somatic SNVs in Cancer}

Calling somatic mutations requires distinguishing true tumor-specific variants from the millions of background germline variants present in the patient. Algorithms like \textbf{VarScan2} accomplish this by processing a tumor sample and a matched normal control sample in parallel.

\subsection{The VarScan2 Heuristic and Fisher's Exact Test}
VarScan2 evaluates the tumor and control samples:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Independent Genotyping}: It first makes a basic genotype call for both tumor and normal based on read thresholds (e.g., requires $\ge 3$ reads with $Q \ge 20$).
    \item \textbf{Comparison}: If the independently called genotypes disagree (e.g., Normal = Homozygous Reference, Tumor = Heterozygous Variant), VarScan2 tests whether the difference in allele counts is statistically significant.
\end{enumerate}

It constructs a $2 \times 2$ contingency table of read counts (Reference vs. Alternate) for both samples, and applies a one-tailed \textbf{Fisher's exact test} utilizing the hypergeometric distribution to find the probability of observing exactly $N_{T,alt}$ alternate reads in the tumor:
\begin{equation}
    P(X = N_{T, alt}) = \frac{\binom{N_T}{N_{T, alt}} \binom{N_C}{N_{C, alt}}}{\binom{N_{tot}}{N_{alt}}}
\end{equation}

Because we want the probability of observing \textit{at least} this many alternate reads by chance, we sum the probabilities from $N_{T,alt}$ to $N_T$:
\begin{equation}
    P(X \ge N_{T, alt}) = \sum_{i=N_{T, alt}}^{N_T} \frac{\binom{N_T}{i} \binom{N_C}{N_{C, alt}}}{\binom{N_{tot}}{i + N_{C, alt}}}
\end{equation}

If the resulting p-value is $\le 0.05$, the variant is officially called a \textit{somatic mutation}.

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Loss of Heterozygosity (LOH)}: A specific somatic event where the normal tissue is heterozygous (e.g., has both an \texttt{A} and a \texttt{T} allele), but the tumor tissue appears homozygous (e.g., only shows \texttt{T}). This often occurs because the genomic segment containing the \texttt{A} allele was physically deleted in the tumor's DNA, leaving only the mutated \texttt{T} allele.
\end{important}

\section{Calling Structural Variations (CNVs) via Read Depth}

To find massive duplications or deletions (Copy Number Variants), we cannot look at single nucleotide pileups. Instead, we analyze \textbf{Read Depth} (coverage) across large consecutive genomic regions.

\subsection{Coverage Plots and the Poisson Distribution}
We divide the genome into fixed-size windows (e.g., 1 kbp or 10 kbp). We count the number of reads mapping to each window.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A normal diploid region (2 copies) serves as the baseline.
    \item A heterozygous deletion (1 copy) will show approximately half the baseline reads.
    \item A duplication (3 copies) will show 1.5$\times$ the baseline reads.
\end{itemize}

Because read mapping is a random process, the expected number of reads mapping to a window follows a Poisson distribution:
\begin{equation}
    P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!} \quad \text{where} \quad \lambda = \frac{N}{G} \cdot W
\end{equation}
Here, $N$ is total reads, $G$ is genome size, and $W$ is window size. 

For large $\lambda$ ($\ge 10$), the Poisson distribution is approximated by a normal distribution, allowing us to compute a \textbf{z-score} for every window:
\begin{equation}
    z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}
\end{equation}
The z-score tells us how many standard deviations a window's read count is above or below the chromosomal mean, which is then converted into an upper-tail or lower-tail p-value.

\subsection{Event-Wise Testing (EWT)}

To prevent calling spurious CNVs from a single noisy window, we search for consecutive windows that consistently show aberrant z-scores. The Event-Wise Testing (EWT) algorithm by Yoon et al. (2009) starts with window size $\ell=1$, then iteratively expands to $\ell=2, 3...$ grouping adjacent windows until the mathematical condition breaks.

An interval $\mathcal{A}$ of $\ell$ windows is called a duplication if the maximum (least significant) p-value inside the interval meets a modified threshold:
\begin{equation}
    \max_i \{ P_i^{\text{Upper}} \mid i \in \mathcal{A} \} < \left( \frac{\ell}{L} \times \text{FPR} \right)^{\frac{1}{\ell}}
\end{equation}
Here, $L$ is the total number of windows on the chromosome, and FPR is the target false positive rate (e.g., 0.05). 

\subsection{Correction for Multiple Testing}

Why does the EWT formula look so complex? It is attempting to solve the \textbf{Multiple Testing Problem}.

\begin{important}
  \textbf{Multiple Testing Correction (MTC)}: A statistical adjustment required when performing millions of simultaneous tests across the genome. If we test 20,000 genes with a significance threshold of $\alpha = 0.05$, we accept a 5\% chance of being wrong on any individual test. Across 20,000 tests, a 5\% error rate guarantees roughly 1,000 false positive discoveries. "If you torture the data long enough, it will confess."
\end{important}

The most stringent MTC is the \textbf{Bonferroni correction}, which divides the target significance level ($\alpha=0.05$) by the total number of tests ($k$) to maintain the overall error rate:
\[ p_{threshold} = \frac{\alpha}{k} \]

For CNV calling, a strict Bonferroni correction for $L$ windows would require $P_i < \frac{\text{FPR}}{L}$. However, this is so conservative that it causes many "false negatives" (missing true CNVs). The EWT formula term $\left( \frac{\ell}{L} \times \text{FPR} \right)^{\frac{1}{\ell}}$ acts as a less restrictive, heuristic relaxation of Bonferroni designed specifically for contiguous, non-overlapping windows.
